Venus at 75 metres

Nobody has ground-truth elevation for Venus at 75 m. ISHTAR is an attempt to recover it anyway — from radar physics, a coarse altimeter, and a stereo DEM covering a fifth of the planet — and to say honestly, pixel by pixel, how much of the result to believe.

The problem is not regression

Magellan mapped Venus at 75 m per pixel, but only in radar brightness. Its altimeter measured elevation on a 4 641 m grid through a footprint about 10 × 20 km across — two orders of magnitude coarser. Between those two products lies a band, roughly 100 m to 10 km, where nothing measured the shape of the planet at all.

So there is no label to regress to. The 75 m signal has to come from physics. A facet tilted toward the radar returns more energy, and the Magellan mosaics are already flattened for the nominal incidence angle — the stored value is RV = 10 log₁₀(σ⁰ / σ⁰Muhleman), encoded as DN = 5(RV + 20) + 1. What is left in that residual is, to first order, exactly the slope. Inverting it is called radarclinometry, and it is ill-posed: the slope along the look direction is well constrained, the slope across it is not constrained at all.

ISHTAR closes that gap with everything else available at once. A network predicts a residual over the upsampled altimetry, and is trained against five things simultaneously: the stereo DEM where it exists, the altimeter seen through its own footprint, a differentiable radar renderer at full resolution, the opposite look where two exist, and the measured roughness. It also predicts its own variance, which is what makes the product honest rather than merely plausible.

What Phase 0 tests, and what it cannot

Everything below runs on a synthetic Venus: fractal terrain parameterised by RMS slope, rendered through the same forward model the losses invert, then degraded the way Magellan degrades — gamma speckle, gain striping, 8-bit quantisation, layover dropouts. The point is that here the answer is known, so a pipeline that cannot recover it will not recover Venus either, and finding that out costs minutes rather than 300 GB and several GPU-days.

It says nothing about Venus. The synthetic set has no mosaic seams, no real layover, no tessera, and no Earth-pretraining stage — which is the premise of the whole approach. Read the numbers as a statement about the code.

The result that mattered

The eight loss terms are not in the same units. L_stereo and L_alt are metres, L_phys is decibels, L_rms is radians. Applied to the raw values, the specification's weights give the radarclinometry term — the only source of sub-kilometre detail across the 80% of the planet with a single look and no stereo — 0.5% of the objective. The roughness term gets 0.0%. Nothing in the loss curve says so. The model simply converges toward a smooth planet, and every number you would think to check looks fine.

Dividing each term by its own observational uncertainty — 75 m for the stereo DEM, 30 m for altimetry, 2 dB for the speckle-limited radiometric residual, one degree for slope and roughness — makes the weights mean what they say, and turns them into a statement of relative trust rather than an accident of units. Four and a half times the skill, and it is not only a matter of score. The raw-units arm is unstable: halfway through it wanders to 244 m — nearly twice the error of the baseline it started from, with a 147 m altimetry residual — and only recovers as the learning rate decays. Gradient norms there sit at 1 000–7 000 against 25–150 for the normalised arm. It also ends up badly over-cautious, reporting a mean 1σ of 255 m and 81% coverage against a 68% target, because the uncertainty term is one of the few that was already dimensionless and is now swamped.

The full table

A single RMSE is not an honest summary of super-resolution without labels, so all of it is reported, against all three baselines. Baseline (b) is the one that matters: it inverts the same forward model with no learned prior at all, so beating it is what justifies the network existing.

Three of the rows are controls rather than candidates. The supervised control fits the true elevation directly on eight tiles: if that failed, the problem would be representation or optimisation rather than supervision, and a flat metric curve looks identical either way. It reaches +77% skill on its own training tiles by step 500 while staying at +5.8% on held-out ones, which settles the question — the architecture has capacity to spare. It also fails calibration instructively: its mean 1σ falls from 100 m to 16 m while held-out error does not improve, so coverage collapses to 9.6%. That is what a memorising model's uncertainty looks like. The pretrain-then-fine-tune pair is the specification's central claim in miniature: learn the prior where a real label exists, then adapt with weak supervision only. On Venus that first stage is Sentinel-1 paired with Copernicus GLO-30; here it stands in as synthetic ground truth, which tests the mechanism without testing the transfer.

The pretrain row is worth reading on its own, because it separates two things a single MAE conflates. Against the weakly supervised run it has the better shape — 2.86° of slope error against 4.70° — and much worse anchoring, 17.2 m of altimetry drift against 10.2 m, because nothing in a supervised-only objective ties the surface to the altimeter. That is precisely the division of labour the staged design assumes: the prior supplies shape, the Venus terms supply constraint.

The supervised pretrain alone wins almost every column, and that is not a result about staging. It is an artefact of the setup: on synthetic data the pretraining label is the test answer, drawn from the same generator, so nothing can beat training directly on it and the Venus terms are a proxy that necessarily pulls away from it. On Venus no such label exists anywhere, which is the entire premise of the project. Reporting this arm as a win would be measuring the experiment, not the method.

What the comparison does support is narrower and more useful. Fine-tuning recovers the anchoring the supervised objective throws away — 10.35 m of altimetry residual against 17.24 m — for about 1.4 m of MAE, and on the real problem that trade is the point, because an unanchored surface drifts globally with no ground truth to catch it. And it retains most of the prior rather than training it away: 3.43° of slope error against the cold start's 4.70°, and 24.78 dB of cross-look against 23.70 dB.

That last number is the sharpest thing in the table. Cross-look PSNR is the honest test of 75 m detail — predict a DEM from one look, render the other look from it, score against an image the model never saw. The cold-start run scores below the bicubic baseline. Left to the Venus terms alone at this training budget, it adds detail that does not predict the other look, while its MAE improves by 20%. Nothing else in the table shows that.

The raw-loss-units row is the loss-balance finding restated by the model itself. It scores +1.0% overall and yet holds the best altimetry residual in the table — 7.6 m against the normalised run's 10.2 m. That is exactly what the diagnosis predicts: with raw scaling the metre-valued terms dominate, so the model becomes excellent at the one thing they measure and mediocre at everything else. Its slope error, 6.75°, is barely better than the bicubic baseline's 7.48°, and its radiometric residual is worse than the baseline's. It optimised the altimeter and ignored the radar.

The physics residual column says the same thing from the other side. Classical radarclinometry has the best of them at 1.40 dB, because it optimises that quantity directly and per tile at test time. The two runs that never see the physics term with any real weight — raw units at 2.33 dB, cold start at 2.32 dB — sit at the bicubic baseline's 2.30 dB. They explain the radar image no better than a surface with no detail in it at all.

The spectrum is the figure that tells the truth

Two models can share an RMSE and be wrong in opposite directions. The radially averaged power spectrum separates them, and it is the reason the specification asks for it rather than another scalar.

Six things that were wrong

Each of these produces a plausible-looking planet rather than an error, which is why each now has a test that fails loudly if it comes back.

Sign convention

The look vector points away from the radar

The specification defines α = atan(∇z · look_vec) as the tilt toward the radar. That identity only holds when look_vec is the down-range beam direction — pointing away. The natural reading, "the vector from the ground to the radar", inverts every slope in the model. The rendered image still looks like SAR, the losses still fall, and the DEM comes out inside-out.

Tiling

The altimeter footprint is bigger than the tile

A 512 px tile at 75 m is 38 km across; the footprint is 10 × 20 km. Convolving with a kernel that size means the boundary condition propagates about 3σ — 25 km — inward, so on a bare tile essentially every altimetry post measures the padding. Measured with a 60 m ripple the footprint should annihilate completely: 4.54 m of leakage on a 256 px tile, 0.113 m at 512 px with a margin, 0.0007 m at 1024 px. Tiles are now cut with a 28.8 km context ring.

The tempting fix — blur the altimetry to match, which cancels the edge effect exactly — is wrong. GTDR already is the footprint-averaged surface, so blurring it again anchors the model to a doubly smoothed target and biases the whole planet smooth, in the one term whose job is to be trustworthy.

Loss construction

Pyramiding the physics loss on the DEM biases it

At 75 m the radiometric residual is dominated by speckle, so the physics term also runs on a 4× pyramid. The obvious implementation downsamples the DEM and renders from it, saving a render. It is biased: backscatter is nonlinear in slope, so the render of a block-mean slope is not the block mean of the render, and the gap grows with subgrid roughness — a systematic error concentrated in tessera, the roughest and most interesting terrain. At the true DEM the biased version bottoms out at 0.27 instead of zero. The pyramid now averages the residual image instead.

Units

The eight loss terms were not commensurable

Covered above — it is the finding that changed the results most, and the only one whose symptom was purely a number that looked fine.

Checkpointing

The saved model was 41% untrained

The trainer logs metrics from the live weights. Everything downstream — evaluation, calibration, the figures on this page, global inference — loads the exponential moving average, because that is what you want at inference time. A fixed decay of 0.999 retains 0.999t of the initial weights, which after 900 steps is 41%.

The residual column is the tell: the average's predicted correction over the altimetry is a seventh of the model's, because the residual head is zero-initialised and the average is still largely holding that zero. Nothing in the training log shows it — the log reports the other set of weights. It surfaced only because a standalone evaluation of the same checkpoint disagreed with a number the trainer had printed twenty minutes earlier. The decay now warms up, and the trainer scores both sets at the end of every run so the two can never quietly diverge again.

Gridding

The altimetry posts were not where the loss looked for them

The altimetry loss samples the fine grid at offset + j × stride — 31, 93, 155, 217 at 75 m with a 62 px stride. A standard interpolate call stretches its input to fill the output instead, putting post j at (j + ½) × H / P − ½. Those agree only when the tile height happens to equal posts × stride, which at these numbers it does not: on a 512 px tile the last post lands about six pixels — 450 m — from where the loss reads it. The anchor was comparing the model at one place against altimetry interpolated from another, a systematic error of order 10 m in the term whose entire job is to stop the surface drifting.

Alongside it: GTDR nodata is −32768, which decodes to 0 m, and Venus has no sea level. The validity mask was being decoded and then dropped, so over the ~2% of the planet the altimeter never measured, the loss would have anchored the surface to zero and the uncertainty head would have had no reason to object.

Both are real-pipeline bugs, found with no real data — by keeping the synthetic generator and the ingest path on one shared definition of where a post is, and testing that definition.

The globe

The product is meant to be looked at, so it ships as a CesiumJS globe on the 6 051.8 km sphere: SAR, learned relief, the uncertainty layer as a confidence fog, a swipe against the altimetry baseline, elevation transects, and a tour. Terrain is quantized-mesh encoded on the Venus sphere rather than by an off-the-shelf tiler — every one of those computes bounding spheres and horizon occlusion points on WGS84, which is culling metadata, so wrong values make tiles vanish at the limb and the failure reads as a renderer bug. On the Venus sphere the encoder's bounding sphere at level 0 comes out at exactly the planet radius, which is the optimum.

What happens next

None of this has touched Magellan yet. The ingest path — grid arithmetic, DN decoding, longitude wrapping, tile assembly — is implemented and tested against in-memory rasters; what is missing is roughly 300 GB of USGS and PDS products and a CUDA machine, since MPS is about 30× worse per pixel above 128 px tiles. In order: clip six regions through Map-A-Planet 2, three of them with stereo coverage; refit the incidence-angle model from the real product labels, because the physics loss reads those angles directly and a systematic few-degree error becomes a systematic slope error; choose the held-out quadrangles; then the Earth pretraining stage, which is the premise of the whole approach and the one thing Phase 0 cannot speak to.

Two new spacecraft — VERITAS and EnVision — will return the first Venus SAR since 1992 in the early 2030s. Until then, this is what is left in the 1992 data.
